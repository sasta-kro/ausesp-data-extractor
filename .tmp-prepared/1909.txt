Abstract

Academic advising is an important process to assist undergraduate
students in their academic life because it provides students the
direction about personal, social, and educational concerns. However, the
traditional process that an advisor manually generates an advising
schedule often ends up with some conflicts with student schedules. To
address this problem effectively and efficiently, this work introduces
the eXtended Flower Pollination Algorithm (XFPA), which extends the
Adapted Flower pollination Algorithm (AFPA) to automatically generate an
optimal academic advising schedule that conforms to availability and/or
preferences of both advisor and students. In particular, this work
redefines a representative of a candidate schedule (solution), adapts
the operators so that candidates’ solutions can exchange information
during evolution steps, and also accepts the hard and soft constraints
when generating an optimal solution. The remedy function is also
introduced to solve the conflict slots that cannot be suitable for them.
Moreover, to further improve the proposed algorithm, XFPA is modified to
enhance the nearly optimal solution generated in the complex datasets.
The experiment results show that enhanced version of XFPA (EXFPA) is
capable of automatically and efficiently generating optimal solutions in
both simple and complex datasets.

building a system can automatically match each other based on the

1.2 Problem Statement

There are some problems that advisors and students are facing. One of
the problems faced by advisors and students is the conflict in time.
Advisors are usually required to open the advising slots according to
their free time. These slots may conflict with the student’s study
schedules. Some students may not get their preferred time slot and need
to request alternative slots than the slots proposed by the advisors.
Then, the advisors are required to open more slots, accordingly.

Another problem is the manual working on the current system. That manual
working includes slot opening and time allocation. During the Advising
Period, Advisors are required to manually open the advising slots for
the students. Advisors need to adjust the slots with their teaching
schedules. However, for the students, they are required to manually
select the slots by themself, which leads to conflicts among students on
the schedule as if to prioritize on a first come first serve basis. This
policy is a big disadvantage for those students who couldn’t allocate
their slot due to unavailability

So, in order to overcome these problems, this work introduces an
algorithm that could help overcome the problems stated above.

3. Theoretical Background

3.1 The Flower Pollination Algorithm (FPA)

Yang developed FPA based on the flower pollination process of the
flowering plants [4]. The FPA behavior conforms to the following four
rules:

1.  Biotic and cross-pollination can be considered part of global
    pollination and pollen carrying pollinators move with levy flight
    behavior.

2.  For local pollination, abiotic and self-pollination are used.

3.  Pollinators, such as insects, can develop flower constancy, which is
    equivalent to a reproduction probability that is proportional to the
    similarity of the two flowers involved.

4.  The interaction or switching of the global and local pollination can
    be controlled by a switch probability p ε [0, 1], with a slight bias
    towards local pollination.

The four rules are used to update the population. Mathematically, the
rules can be given by the following equation:

  X_(i)^(t + 1) = X_(i)^(t) + L(g^(*) − X₁^(t)) (1)

Equation (1) represents rule 1 including its flower constancy
characteristic, wherein pollinators like the bees and insects travel a
long distance to carry the flower pollen, g^(*)is the current best
solution found among all the solution in the current
iteration/generation, X_(i)^(t) is pollen i at iteration t. The
parameter L represents the strength of the pollination, which is the
step size to mimic the pollinator’s long-distance movement efficiently
[2,3]. Levy distribution is used to imitate this characteristic. L > 0
is derived from a levy distribution,

$L \sim \frac{\lambda\Gamma sin(\frac{\lambda\pi}{2})}{\pi}\ \frac{1}{s^{1 + \lambda}},(s > > \ s_{0}\  > > \ 0)$,
(2)

where λΓ is the standard gamma function. This distribution equation is
valid for large steps where s > 0.

The local pollination rule 2 and its flower constancy characteristic can
be presented as:

  X_(i)^(t + 1) = X_(i)^(t) + U(X_(j)^(t) − X_(k)^(t)), (3)

where X_(j)^(t) − X_(k)^(t) are pollen from other flowers of the same
plant species. It equivalently becomes the local random walk if U is
drawn from a uniform distribution  ∈ [0, 1]. Neighboring flowers are
likely to be pollinated by the local flower pollens than those that are
far away. In order to mimic this characteristic, a switch probability
(rule 4) p is used. From Yang’s work a p equal to 0.8 works better in
many applications [4].

The AFPA was shown in [7] that the flower pollination algorithm could be

lecturer-classroom assignment. The datasets used in this study were a
list of lecturers, a list of courses, a minimum or the maximum number of
unique courses, or the total number of course preparation that a
lecturer-classroom assignment. The datasets used in this study were a

4.2 The eXtended Flower Pollination Algorithm (XFPA)

After identifying the prescribed courses for both advisors and students
and the maximum advising period for each time slot, AFPA then generates
advising timetables for both advisors and students. However, the initial
implementation of AFPA is not applicable to solve the automated
scheduling of advising reservation problems because the course-lecturer
problem, solved by AFPA, and this problem have different data structures
and constraints. Therefore, we introduce in our proposed algorithm XFPA
additional operators and also a modified version of the genetic
operators.The XFPA aims to solve the advisor L and student S advising
time slot assignment problem. It needs the students and the advisor’s
time slot to generate the result. Each advisor is assigned 30 students.
Firstly, the student and advisor can set their preference constraints
individually. Then, it generates a population of flowers with a random
time slot. Each time slot of the student should not conflict with each
other if they are under the same advisor. The solution can be
represented as a combinatorial of Advisor L and student S, where a
feasible outcome will be represented as an advising reservation table
artbl. During the optimization process, the XFPA generates a population,
which contains a finite set of solutions: artbl = {X₁, X₂..., X_(n)},
and each solution X_(i) = (L X S) is provided in Figure 3, where n
stands for the maximum student advising assignment per advisor and m
represents the total number of advisors.

  ------------------------ ------------------------ ---------------------------
  L₁= S₁, S₂, ..., S_(n)   L₂= S₁, S₂, ..., S_(n)   L_(m)= S₁, S₂, ..., S_(n)

  ------------------------ ------------------------ ---------------------------

Figure 3. Representation of a flower X_(i)in advising slot assignment

Then, it finds the best solution in the population by getting the
solution with the highest fitness value. The fitness value fv is
formulated in Eq. 4, where the computed result is equal to 1.0 indicates
an optimal solution in the population.

$fv\  = \ 1.0\  - (\ \frac{\Sigma t_{conflict}}{t_{student}}$) (4)

The t_(conflict) stands for the total number of conflicts when assigning
available slots of advisor to students, t_(student) represents the total
number of students. Eq. 5 describes the minimization of the constraints.

f(X) = minimized(m, q, X_(i)(L × S)) (5)

where i = 1,2…, n is the solution of each flower, m is the slot
preference of the advisor, q is the slot preference of the student
X_(i) = (L × S)and represents the solution of the individual flower
subject to the constraint in m and q .

In the XFPA, unique slots are generated randomly for each advisor. The
selection operator is used to get the fittest flower solution, the
random flower solution, and the current flower solution. Additional
operators are introduced, by providing different variants of the
mutation and crossover operators of the AFPA to cope with the data
structure and constraints of the advising registration problem.

rand < p (6)

The flower constancy is dependent on the random value rand, and the
switch probability p whose relationship is specified in Eq. 6. These
variables determine which pollination process to execute or to switch
between the global pollination and local pollination process. Different
switch probability values (0.1, 0.3, 0.5, 0.7, and 0.9) were used in
this study to determine the performance of the proposed algorithm if
decreasing or increasing p will help achieve the global optimum earlier
or provide a better convergence rate.

In Figure 4, to determine the step size of the pollination in the global
and local pollination Δ_(gp) and Δ_(lp)is derived by getting the
difference of the fitness values of two flower solutions and a set of
parameters pr₁, pr₂, pr₃, pr₄and pr₅ are used to determine which
operator the XFPA will select. To provide the step size, we utilize five
variations of the genetic algorithm operators. For the mutation
variations: the mutationOneClass operator performs mutation on all the
conflict slots while in the mutationTwoClass operator, it swaps the
conflicting slot with a random slot, for the mutationThree-Class
operator it swaps two conflict slots. For the crossover variations; the
crossoverHalfAdvisorload, it uses one advisor and using half of the time
slot assigned in the advisor.Another crossover operator variation is the
crossoverHalfnAdvisorload, where more than one advisor is used, and half
of the time slots assigned are used to exchange the information. In the
crossoverAdvisorload, it is the same with the
crossoverHalfn-Advisorload, but it utilizes all time slots assigned. It
is noted that the XFPA will find an optimal solution for each lecturer
L_(i) at a time. Therefore, the algorithm needs to execute m times to
generate optimal solutions for all lecturers.

|   1 Create a population of n flowers with random solutions,     |

4.3 The Enhancement of the XFPA (EXFPA)

In order to solve the problem stated above the algorithm has been
modified so that our algorithm could reach the optimum value (fv = 1.0).
In the above algorithm, the specified parameters are only used in order
to determine the operator to be used. Such procedures restrict on
choosing the operator to be used in order to produce the new flower.
Therefore, to overcome the restriction of restricting the selection of
the operators, the Enhancement of XFPA (EXFPA) introduces the
randomization of the operator selection. The selection of the operator
will also rely on 3 groups of parameters. The first group is designed in
such a way that the probability of first 2 Mutation is more than other
operators .The second group is designed in such a way that the
probability of getting selected of the third and the fourth type of
operators is high . then the last group is designed in a way that the
probability of operator 4 and 5 is high. The selection of the group
would be based a set of parameters pr₁, pr₂, and pr₃ .Those parameters
could be adjusted accordingly based on the scope of the work.

As shown in the experiment section later, the EXFPA is able to achieve
the optimum value (fv = 1.0) even on the most complex dataset providing
there is at least a matching slot in the preference slot provided by the
lecturer and the students. However, if there really exists no matching
slot provided by the student and teacher, then we can perform the remedy
function introduced in the section 4.2

5. Experiments and Discussions

5.1 Experiments on the XFPA with Complex Datasets

To prove the performance of the XFPA, there are various soft constraints
from advisors and students. For advisors, the preferences of advising
slots are 43 and 30. For students, the preference of slots are 15, 10
and 5. There are different complex datasets with those preferences to
measure the nature of the algorithm. By making pairs of advisors and
students, there will be six sets of soft constraints with different
levels of complexity from least complex to most complex, such as 43
advisor timeslots and 15 student timeslots (43:15), 43 advisor timeslots
and 10 student timeslots (43:10), 43 advisor timeslots and 5 student
timeslots (43:5), 30 advisor timeslots and 15 student timeslots (30:15),
30 advisor timeslots and 10 student timeslots (30:10), and 30 advisor
timeslots and 5 student timeslots (30:5), respectively. The higher the
number of advisors’ and students’ timeslots, the lesser the level of
complexity. The value of the switch probability and the population size
are varied to observe which one can give the best solution. The switch
probability values will be 0.1, 0.3, 0.5, 0.7 and 0.9. The experimental
results are evaluated with fitness value fv. The number of iterations
for all test cases is set to be 1000.

With XFPA, there are several cases that can generate nearly optimal
solutions in Figure 11,12 and 13. The remedy function is applied to
solve the conflict cases. Figure 15 describes the preference table
generated after applying the remedy function. In this given table, one
advisor does not match with three preferred student timeslots or all
preferred students are not available in the advisor’s preference slots.
In Line (1) of Figure 15, an advisor has eight available timeslots.
After applying soft constraints, the advisor will have four remaining
timeslots (line 2). Line 3, 4 and 5 indicate that each student will have
three available timeslots and Line 6, 7 and 8 represent one preferred
timeslot for each student. While performing the matching student
timeslot with advisor, student #1 and student #2 who have the same
preferred timeslot {2} (Line 6 and 7) cause conflict with the advisor's
preferred timeslot. For student #3, his preferred timeslot (Line 8) is
{5} which is not available in advisor’s preferred list (Line 2). With
these cases, the advisor can do advising with one student only. The
non-availability of timeslots for other students will affect the
algorithm not to get an optimal solution even though the population size
and iteration are increasing. With these problems, the remedy function
is to help the algorithm avoid the non-availability of timeslot problem.
It gives a recommended timeslot {3, 2, 5} for both advisor and students
(Line 9), in which timeslot {2} can be considered as the best time slot
for both advisor and students based on their preferences. The timeslot
{3, 5} will be a remedy for other students. And then, the non-preferred
time slots of students are generated {2, 6, 5} (Line 10) as a
recommended time slot for all students. It is acquired by getting the
best slot for advisor and students {2} (Line 9) and student’s
non-preference timeslot (Line 3,4 and 5). The non-preference timeslot of
the advisor is generated {2, 6, 5} (Line 11) by utilizing the best slot
for advisor and students {2} (Line 9) and advisor’s non-preference
timeslot (Line 1). With the suggested non-preference list for advisor
and students, the advisor’s non-preference slot {2, 6} can be allocated
to students whose preference is either 2 or 6 (Student #1 and Student
#2) and student #3 will use the non-preferred slot 5 as an alternative
slot. With the suggested time slots of the remedy function, it gives
both advisors and students a back-up time slot if their preferred time
slot is or is not available in their preference lists.

7. Conclusion

The objective of this project is to automatically generate the advising
reservation for undergraduate students using Extended Flower Pollination
Algorithms. The system is able to analyze the timetable of advisors and
students and find the available time slots of both sides. It can allow
them to select their preferred time slots to test the nature of the
algorithms. The benefit of the system is that advisors do not need to
manually open the advising slots for students and students also do not
need to select the slots by themselves. It can save time to wait for
opening slots and the student will no longer be conflicted with their
study schedule. However, t he limitation of this work is that it needs
to have a diverse population in order to get better results, which will
take a lot of iterations in the most complex datasets. The future work,
the system can be implemented in Assumption University. Both advisors
and students can set their preferences and then the advising schedule
can be generated accordingly. They do not need to find and wait for
manual process. Moreover, in the future the work can be extended by
analyzing how we can make the most complex dataset to run faster in
smaller population size
